What are vertical drama series? They are serialized, scripted stories made for a phone: full-screen 9:16 video, episodes of roughly 60 to 120 seconds, and a cliffhanger at the end of most episodes. The category also goes by microdrama, short drama and mini drama, and the format is the same under each name. How viewers pay for these series varies far more than the format does, and that is where most explainers say little. Shortee is a free, ad-supported app. Every series is free to watch with ads, from the first episode to the last, and viewers who do not want ads can subscribe.

## What vertical drama series are

One explainer from [Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/use-cases/microdrama) describes a microdrama as a vertical drama series made for a phone. Episodes run 60 to 120 seconds, they are shot 9:16 full-screen, and a season runs 60 to 100 episodes, usually ending on a cliffhanger. The same source says short drama, mini drama and vertical drama are one format.

[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microdrama) gives matching numbers. The most commercially successful microdramas have episodes of 60 to 120 seconds and 60 to 100 episodes per series, so a complete series runs roughly 90 to 180 minutes in total. It adds that the format originated in China, where it is called duanju.

Those two descriptions agree, and the agreement matters. A vertical drama series is a feature-length story cut into very short chapters, each built to make the next one hard to skip. That structure is also what makes the pricing model in this category so consequential: the cliffhanger arrives just before the point where many apps ask for payment.

Shortee streams serialized short drama shot for the phone, in genres including romance, thriller and comedy. Titles such as [*Single Record*](https://myshortee.com/series/single-record/) are free to watch with ads from the first episode to the last, so the cliffhanger leads straight into the next episode instead of a purchase screen.

## How big the category is, and why the figures disagree

Market-size numbers for vertical drama series are often quoted side by side even though they measure different things. [Omdia](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260222198738/en/Microdramas-Overtake-Streamers-on-Mobile-Engagement-Says-Omdia) estimated global microdrama revenue at $11 billion in 2025, growing to $14 billion by the end of 2026. About $3 billion of that is outside China, and the US is the largest international market. Omdia expects the US to account for 50% of revenue outside China in 2026, reaching $1.5 billion.

Viewing time shows why advertisers and investors pay attention. Omdia's analysis of mobile usage data, reported by [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/microdramas-overtake-streamers-mobile-engagement-061100325.html), shows US users spend more time per day watching microdramas on mobile apps than watching Netflix, Disney+ or Amazon Prime Video on mobile.

Sensor Tower's figures use a narrower scope. In Q1 2026, per [Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/state-of-short-drama-apps-2026-report), DramaBox and ReelShort remained the top-grossing short drama apps, each generating close to US$140M in in-app revenue. [ReelPulse](https://reelpulse.net/industry/market-reports/q1-2026-revenue), citing the same Sensor Tower report, puts Q1 2026 downloads above 850 million worldwide and in-app purchase revenue at roughly $750 million.

The scope is the catch. According to ReelPulse, Sensor Tower's figures cover App Store and Google Play in-app purchases only. They exclude advertising, web checkout and third-party Android stores, so they understate apps that make money from ads rather than purchases. Another tally, from DataEye via [Muvpix](https://muvpix.com/blogs/global-vertical-microdrama-market), estimates about 1.44 billion overseas short-drama app downloads in the first half of 2026 and about $1.27 billion in app-store in-app purchase revenue outside China. Different publishers, different periods and different scopes mean these totals should not be stacked into one series.

For a viewer, the practical reading is that most published revenue data describes purchases, not advertising. Shortee sits on the advertising side, where a whole series is free to watch with ads and the purchase tallies capture none of that viewing.

## How paid apps charge: free episodes, then coins

The typical paid model is easy to describe and hard to price, because published figures conflict. A monetisation explainer from [CRM.com](https://www.crm.com/microdrama-short-drama-monetisation) says the first 1 to 10 episodes are free, then a cliffhanger paywall appears. After that, coins cost roughly $0.10 to $0.20 per episode, sold in packs from about $4.99 to $99.99. Rewarded ads are typically capped at about five a day, after which the only way forward is to pay.

A second analysis, from [Spyrosoft](https://spyro-soft.com/blog/media-and-entertainment/microdrama-monetisation-models-why-the-real-innovation-isnt-the-content-its-the-payment-stack), estimates 50 to 200 coins per episode, about $0.10 to $0.20, and says a full 80-episode series costs a viewer $30 to $50 in aggregate when unlocked with coins. It notes that few viewers do that calculation.

A third source puts DramaBox higher. [Triple Minds](https://tripleminds.co/blogs/strategies/dramabox-business-model-funding) says roughly the first 5 to 10 episodes are free, then episodes cost about 60 to 120 coins, roughly $0.40 to $0.80, each, with weekly ad-free passes at $17.99 to $19.99. It describes these as ranges that are A/B-tested and shift by region.

The spread between $0.10 to $0.20 and $0.40 to $0.80 per episode is the reason a viewer cannot know the cost of finishing a story before starting it. Prices that are A/B-tested and vary by region mean two viewers can pay different amounts for the same episode.

Shortee removes that calculation. Its pricing model is free with ads for the whole series, with no coin packs standing between the viewer and the ending.

## What viewers complain about: billing

The complaint data points in one direction. A review-analysis site, [Unstar](https://unstar.app/blog/is-dramabox-legit-safe-short-drama-app-reviews-2026), found that the most common complaint in DramaBox 1-star reviews, at 30%, is the weekly price, with reviewers quoting $16.99 to $19.99 per week. Coins that drain, expire or disappear are the next most common complaint, at 20%.

These are review samples, which skew toward unhappy customers, so they show what goes wrong rather than how often. Still, the complaint is consistent across three independent sources: the problem is the charging, not the stories.
