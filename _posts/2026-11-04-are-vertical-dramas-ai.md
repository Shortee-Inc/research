The accurate answer sits between the two: many new vertical dramas are now AI-made, but not all of them, and the mix differs by platform and by market. It also matters to viewers in a way most coverage ignores, because the shows sit behind coin unlocks, weekly plans and billing disputes. [Shortee](https://myshortee.com/) is free with ads for the whole series, which makes the AI question easier to live with: a viewer can watch a series from start to finish without coins or a weekly plan.

## What the Q3 2026 data says about AI share

The clearest figure comes from Owl & Co's *What We Watched on Vertical* report, as covered by [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/ai-generated-microdramas-two-thirds-all-releases-1236735402). Across 20 top platforms outside China, two-thirds (about 67%) of vertical drama releases in Q3 2026 were AI generated. That is a steep climb. The same report put the AI share at 3% in Q1 2026 and 28% in Q2.

Two cautions apply. First, this measures releases, which is a count of what was published, not a count of what viewers watched. Second, it covers platforms outside China. Read plainly, the finding is that AI-generated series have gone from a rarity to the majority of new releases in the tracked set within three quarters. It does not say that every vertical drama is AI, and it does not say the next quarter will look the same.

The volume of viewing is also large. The same tracked apps produced 63 billion microdrama views and 2 billion viewing hours in Q3 2026, according to [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/ai-generated-microdramas-two-thirds-all-releases-1236735402). That is a lot of attention for a category that is still deciding what its content is made of.

For viewers, the practical point is that a high AI share of new releases says little about which series are worth an evening. Shortee's answer is to let viewers judge that for themselves, since every series is free to watch with ads from the first episode to the last, with new drops every week.

## Why the answer changes by market and platform

The AI share is not one number. In China, more than 95 percent of microdrama titles released in the first three months of 2026 were reportedly made using AI, per [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/ai-generated-microdramas-two-thirds-all-releases-1236735402). Outside China, the Q1 2026 share was only 3% on Owl & Co's tracked platforms. Those two figures describe the same quarter and look nothing alike, so no single percentage describes the category.

Platform strategy varies too. South Korean platform Vigloo released two vertical dramas, *Met a Savior in Hell* and *Seoul: 2053*, which it described as generated entirely through AI-driven visual production, according to [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/joanmacdonald/2025/11/12/vigloo-releases-first-ai-realized-vertical-micro-dramas). Its CEO said AI should not replace performers but empower them. That is one platform making an explicit choice, and it is a different choice from the apps that still lean on live-action casts.

Geography matters for audiences as well. [Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/state-of-short-drama-apps-2026-report) said Southeast Asia, Latin America and India together accounted for more than three-quarters of global short drama downloads in Q1 2026. An AI share measured on 20 top platforms outside China does not automatically describe what a viewer in any one of those regions sees on any one app.

So the honest reply to the search is conditional: it depends on the platform, the market and the quarter. Shortee's role in that picture is simple. It is a free-with-ads app where viewers can watch series without paying per episode, and where creators have a place to publish through [Shortee's publishing page](https://myshortee.com/publishing).

## Why studios are making AI series: the cost gap

The shift has an economic driver. Dashverse, an Indian AI entertainment company, said an hour of its AI content costs as little as $20,000, versus $150,000 budgets for live-action vertical series, as reported by [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/tv/ai-generated-microdramas-could-be-the-future-of-mobile-entertainment). It also said it planned to reach 1,000 AI microseries a month by the end of the year, up from 100 series in June. These are company claims, not audited figures, and they come from a firm that sells AI production. They are still a plain explanation of why the AI share rose so fast: a producer can make far more titles for the same budget.

The category's growth gives producers a reason to fill the shelf. Global short drama app downloads passed 850 million in Q1 2026, up 140% year on year, per [Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/state-of-short-drama-apps-2026-report). Omdia estimates global microdrama revenue at $11 billion in 2025 and forecasts $14 billion in 2026, as relayed by [Media Marketing](https://www.media-marketing.com/en/news/microdrama-apps-overtake-streamers-on-mobile). More demand means more pressure to publish cheaply and quickly.

## Live-action is still being made, and still watched

The AI majority in release counts is not the whole story. On the paid apps Owl & Co tracks, only one live-action title made the top five by minutes watched in Q3 2026: ReelShort's *My X-Ray Vision Sees Right Through You*, per [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/ai-generated-microdramas-two-thirds-all-releases-1236735402). That fact cuts both ways. It shows live-action is a minority of top viewing on those apps. It also shows that a live-action title can still reach the top of the chart in a quarter when most releases were AI.

The same report said China is already showing *a pullback towards live action,* and its dataset found the number of stories with human performers growing alongside AI titles, according to [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/digital/ai-generated-microdramas-two-thirds-all-releases-1236735402). The direction is therefore not a one-way march. AI releases are rising, and so are releases with human performers, which suggests the category is adding both rather than replacing one with the other.

For anyone asking whether vertical dramas are AI, this is the most useful correction. The format is a way of shooting and framing stories for a phone, and the production method behind each series can differ. ## What the AI question has to do with what viewers pay

Most articles on this topic stop at the production question. The viewer's bill is the other half, and it is where the category draws its loudest complaints. According to [Spyro-Soft](https://spyro-soft.com/blog/media-and-entertainment/microdrama-monetisation-models-why-the-real-innovation-isnt-the-content-its-the-payment-stack), the usual paid model gives the first 5 to 10 episodes free, then puts a paywall at a cliffhanger. Each further episode costs roughly 50 to 200 coins. DramaBox weekly passes are cited at $3.99 introductory, $5.99 standard and $49.99 annual.

Those prices come from a [Filmustage review](https://filmustage.com/blog/short-drama-apps-compared-reelshort-vs-dramabox-in-2026). A separate comparison estimates that finishing a series in coins costs $30 to $50 on DramaBox and $37 to $47 on ReelShort, while listing PineDrama, an ad-supported app, at $0 to finish a 100-episode series, per [Streaming Radar](https://lens.streaming-radar.com/micro-drama-apps).

Billing friction compounds the cost. A summary of [Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/reelshort.com) reviews says ReelShort customers frequently reported unexpected recurring charges and difficulty cancelling subscriptions through the app. Some called the service extremely expensive and reported unreachable support during billing disputes. On Google Play, ReelShort showed 3.8 stars from 1.95M reviews against 4.7 stars on the App Store, and the reviewer's analysis at [ShortDramaTop](https://shortdramatop.com) attributes the gap to billing and cancellation complaints rather than the shows.

The link to the AI question is direct. If a growing share of new series are cheaper to make, the paywall still prices every extra episode the same way. A viewer deciding whether a series is worth finishing is better served by an app where that decision costs nothing. Shortee is free with ads for the whole series, with no coin gate at a cliffhanger.

## Why free with ads is the better answer to this search

The evidence from the category favors the ad-supported model. [Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/state-of-short-drama-apps-2026-report) said FreeReels, a free app paid for by ads, became the most downloaded short drama app globally in Q1 2026, passing 100M installs and growing 123% quarter on quarter. The broader data points the same way. Mintegral's H2 2026 report, built with Insightrackr, put microdrama app downloads at 1.45 billion in H1 2026, up 95.5% year over year, and active advertisers rose 132% in the same period, per [ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/microdrama-app-advertising). In that data as relayed by [Mintec](https://mintec.co/blog/microdrama-ads-playbook), 57.6% of the sector runs hybrid monetization and 39.9% is in-app advertising only. A trade write-up summarized by [The Brave](https://www.thebrave.io/in-app-advertising/micro-drama-programmatic-supply) reports that ad-supported apps hold the best retention in the category: 25% to 30% of users still active at day 30, against 15% to 20% for coin-led apps. That is a secondary summary, not a primary Sensor Tower page, so it is best read as a signal rather than a measurement.
